\section{USB-PD}\label{sec:usb-pd}

This section derives the input stage that supplies all three converters: the
Type-C receptacle, the port controller that negotiates a contract on it, the
switch that connects the negotiated rail to the board, and the data path that
reaches the MCU's native USB. It is built around the CYPD3176
(\cref{sec:cypd3176}), referred to by part number throughout,
being the only device of its kind on the board. Unlike the two converter
subsystems, whose driving-component characteristics are collected ahead of
their derivations in \cref{sec:hv-components,sec:lv-components}, this
subsystem's are gathered in its own first subsection, there being one such

\subsection{Load budget referred to the bus}\label{sec:pd-load}

The three converters' input powers, each at the worst case its own section
derives:
\begin{align*}
  P_{\mathrm{in,HV}} &= \SI{3.54}{\watt} && \text{(\cref{sec:hv-op}, design-max envelope, } \eta = 0.833), \\
  P_{\mathrm{in,5V}} &= \SI{0.588}{\watt} && \text{(\cref{sec:5v-ss}, \SI{0.50}{\watt} at } \eta = 0.85), \\
  P_{\mathrm{in,3V3}} &= \SI{1.39}{\watt} && \text{(\cref{sec:3v3-load}, \SI{1.18}{\watt} at } \eta = 0.85),
\end{align*}
summing worst-case additive to \SI{5.51}{\watt}. Referred to each accepted
contract at its $-5\,\%$ limit, and adding the controller's own
\SI{10}{\milli\ampere}:
\begin{align*}
  I_{\mathrm{port}} &= \frac{5.51}{V_{\mathrm{BUS}}} + 0.010
  &&\SI{4.75}{\volt}: \SI{1.170}{\ampere}; \quad
    \SI{8.55}{\volt}: \SI{0.654}{\ampere}; \quad
    \SI{11.4}{\volt}: \SI{0.493}{\ampere}.
\end{align*}
The \SI{4.75}{\volt} figure governs every conducted-current requirement in this
section, being the bottom of the \SIrange{4.75}{13}{\volt} window
\cref{sec:hv-converter} derives against. Worst-case additive
overstates the real draw: the flyback's design-max envelope puts every digit
tube simultaneously at its datasheet-maximum current, and the 3.3~V figure is
the MCU's Wi-Fi transmit condition at \SI{100}{\percent} duty. Neither is a
sustained operating point and their coincidence is not one either, but the
front end is sized against it because a source that cannot supply it will not
negotiate at all.

\subsection{Contract window}\label{sec:pd-window}

VBUS\_MIN and VBUS\_MAX each take one of six settings through a divider from
VDDD (\ds{infineon-cypd3176-24lqxq-datasheet-en.pdf}{13}), listed in
\cref{tab:pd-vbus-settings}. The pair defines the range of source voltages the
controller will accept.

\centering

\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
\textbf{Voltage (V)} & \textbf{Ratio to VDDD} & \textbf{Pull-up (\si{\kilo\ohm})} & \textbf{Pull-down (\si{\kilo\ohm})} & \textbf{Pin window (mV)} \\
\hline
5 & 0/6 & open & 0 & 0--248 \\
\hline
9 & 1/6 & 5.1 & 1 & 249--786 \\
\hline
12 & 2/6 & 5.1 & 2.4 & 787--1347 \\
\hline
15 & 3/6 & 5.1 & 5.1 & 1348--1920 \\
\hline

\hline
20 & $\geq$5/6 & 0 & open & $\geq$2779 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Upper limit.} \SI{15}{\volt} is the first setting above
\SI{12}{\volt} and it exceeds what the downstream converters tolerate. A
\SI{15}{\volt} contract at its $+5\,\%$ limit is \SI{15.75}{\volt}, which is
\SI{87.5}{\percent} of the TPS629203's \SI{18}{\volt} absolute maximum
(\cref{sec:tps629203}) and past the \SI{80}{\percent} derating this document
applies to voltage ratings throughout. Specification: VBUS\_MAX =
\SI{12}{\volt}, which at $+5\,\%$ gives \SI{12.6}{\volt} and holds the
\SI{13}{\volt} ceiling that \cref{sec:hv-converter,sec:5v-converter,sec:3v3-converter}
derive against.

\paragraph{Lower limit.} A window whose floor is \SI{9}{\volt} leaves the board
dead on any source that cannot offer a high-voltage contract, including an
ordinary host port. The board must come up on such a source so the MCU can be
programmed and reached over USB, which \cref{sec:pd-data} routes. Setting the
floor to \SI{5}{\volt} admits those sources, and does so at no component cost:
the \SI{5}{\volt} entry is the 0/6 ratio, an open pull-up and a pull-down of
zero, which is a direct connection to ground. Specification:
VBUS\_MIN = \SI{5}{\volt}, tied to ground; VBUS\_MAX = \SI{12}{\volt} through
\des{R}{PDMAX1} $= \SI{5.1}{\kilo\ohm}$ to VDDD and \des{R}{PDMAX2} $=
\SI{2.4}{\kilo\ohm}$ to ground.

The accepted set is therefore \SI{5}{\volt}, \SI{9}{\volt}, and
\SI{12}{\volt}. \emph{Assumption:} presented with a source offering more than
one of them, the controller requests the highest. The datasheet states that
VBUS\_MIN and VBUS\_MAX bound what the controller will accept but never states
the selection rule within those bounds. The assumption is what VBUS\_MAX is for,
and its consequence is bounded twice over: \cref{sec:pd-sequence} recovers the
highest offered voltage by renegotiation at the cost of one voltage transition,
and a board left holding a \SI{5}{\volt} contract still reaches full operation
from any source that grants the current \cref{sec:pd-classes} requires there.

\subsection{Operating current request}\label{sec:pd-isnk}

ISNK\_COARSE and ISNK\_FINE are read the same way and sum to the requested
operating current (\ds{infineon-cypd3176-24lqxq-datasheet-en.pdf}{13, 14}):
coarse in \SI{1}{\ampere} steps from \SIrange{0}{5}{\ampere}, fine in the
sequence \SI{0}{}, \SI{250}{}, \SI{500}{}, \SI{750}{}, \SI{900}{\milli\ampere}.
The requirement is that the request admit the weakest source the board must
start on while covering the board's own draw there, since every increment
above it narrows the set of sources that can satisfy it: a source
offering a voltage inside the window but a current below the request is a fault
condition, not a degraded contract
(\ds{infineon-cypd3176-24lqxq-datasheet-en.pdf}{14}).

A single setting cannot serve both operating classes. Covering full operation
takes the \SI{750}{\milli\ampere} step at \SI{9}{\volt} and more than the fine
sequence reaches at \SI{5}{\volt} (\cref{tab:pd-required}), and a source
offering \SI{5}{\volt} at \SI{500}{\milli\ampere}, which is
what a USB~2.0 host port supplies, would then be refused: its voltage is inside
the window and its current is below the request, so the contract does not close
and the pass switch never asserts. That is the source \cref{sec:pd-window}
widened the window to admit, and a request sized for full operation would leave
the board dead on it.

The resistors therefore ask for what limited operation needs, and firmware asks
for the rest once it is running (\cref{sec:pd-sequence}).
\Cref{sec:pd-classes} puts limited operation at \SI{0.404}{\ampere}, so the
\SI{500}{\milli\ampere} fine step covers it with \SI{24}{\percent} to spare and
matches the weakest port exactly. Specification: ISNK\_COARSE =
\SI{0}{\ampere}, tied to ground; ISNK\_FINE = $+\SI{500}{\milli\ampere}$
through \des{R}{PDI1} $= \SI{5.1}{\kilo\ohm}$ to VDDD and \des{R}{PDI2} $=
\SI{2.4}{\kilo\ohm}$ to ground.

At the three accepted voltages this asks \SI{2.5}{\watt}, \SI{4.5}{\watt}, and
\SI{6}{\watt}, all of which any source offering the corresponding contract
supplies, the smallest common USB-PD adapter supplying \SI{9}{\volt} at
\SI{2}{\ampere} and a bare host port \SI{5}{\volt} at
\SI{500}{\milli\ampere}. What it does not cover is the display, and nothing in
the resistor settings prevents the display from starting on it, which is what
\cref{sec:pd-sequence} has to arrange.

\subsection{Operating classes}\label{sec:pd-classes}

The window of \cref{sec:pd-window} admits results at three voltages, and every
converter is derived to run at all of them: the flyback across
\SIrange{4.75}{13}{\volt} (\cref{sec:hv-converter}), the 5~V rail in regulation
above \SI{8.5}{\volt} and tracking its input below (\cref{sec:5v-limited}), the
3.3~V rail across every band (\cref{sec:3v3-converter}). What separates the
design's two operating classes is therefore not the voltage of the result but
the current behind it: whether the source guarantees the operating current the
display's class draws at that voltage.

\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|}

\paragraph{Full operation.} A result that guarantees the request of
\cref{tab:pd-required} at its voltage. On a PD contract the guarantee is the
contract itself, raised from the attach request to the table's figure by the
renegotiation of \cref{sec:pd-sequence}. The paths that bypass PD carry their
guarantees differently: a BC1.2 charging port floors at \SI{1.5}{\ampere} by
that specification; a QC~2.0 or AFC result at \SI{9}{\volt} or \SI{12}{\volt}
carries those protocols' ampere-class minimums, verified against the measured
bus voltage; a Type-C-only source advertising \SI{1.5}{\ampere} or
\SI{3}{\ampere} covers the \SI{5}{\volt} requirement directly. In every case
all three converters operate inside their derived envelopes, the firmware
releases the inhibit of \cref{sec:hv-inhibit}, and the display runs. On a
\SI{5}{\volt} result the drivers switch their rated cathode currents from a
rail that tracks the bus; \cref{sec:5v-limited} bounds what that supply
excursion costs.

\paragraph{Limited operation.} Every result short of the table: a
default-current Type-C port, any attach contract before renegotiation, a
source that refuses the raised request, or a proprietary result whose current
floor is unknowable. The inhibit stays asserted, the flyback does not run, the
K155ID1 packages idle with open-collector outputs and no anode supply behind
them, and the 3.3~V rail regulates as everywhere. The board is a running clock
controller without a lit display, which is the intended state on the host port
it is programmed from.

The load in limited operation is the 3.3~V rail and the idle drivers:
\begin{align*}
  I_{\mathrm{port}}^{\mathrm{lim}} &= \frac{1.39}{4.72} + 0.100 + 0.010
    = \SI{0.404}{\ampere},
\end{align*}
the first term at the converter input node, which \des{Q}{3V3BUS} places
\SI{31}{\milli\volt} below the bottom of the range where a diode would have cost
a forward drop (\cref{sec:3v3-input}), the second the drivers passed
through at unity duty, the third the
controller. That is \SI{81}{\percent} of the \SI{500}{\milli\ampere} a
USB~2.0 host port supplies and well inside both a USB~3.x port's
\SI{900}{\milli\ampere} and a Type-C source's default
\SI{1.5}{\ampere}. Limited operation is therefore self-consistent on the
weakest source the board will encounter, and it is so at the 3.3~V rail's
worst-case load, not merely at a programming-time load.

\subsection{Start-up and renegotiation}\label{sec:pd-sequence}

The contract the resistors ask for carries the board but not the display
(\cref{sec:pd-isnk}). Closing that gap without a power cycle is a firmware
sequence, and it turns on two constraints: the display must not start on a
contract that cannot carry it, and the bus voltage must not step while the MCU
depends on it.

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Attach.} The controller runs from the receptacle's VBUS through
    its own regulator and negotiates the highest voltage in the window at
    \SI{500}{\milli\ampere}. VBUS\_CTRL closes under the slew control of
    \cref{sec:pd-fet}.
  \item \textbf{Rails.} The 3.3~V rail comes up and the MCU boots; the 5~V rail
    comes up and the drivers idle. The flyback stays off behind the inhibit of
    \cref{sec:hv-inhibit}, which no contract voltage bypasses.
  \item \textbf{Interrogate.} The MCU reads the negotiated result, the active
    legacy protocol, and the measured bus voltage over HPI
    (\cref{sec:pd-pins}).
  \item \textbf{Renegotiate.} On a PD contract short of full operation, the MCU
    requests the operating current \cref{tab:pd-required} assigns that
    contract's voltage, covering the referred draw with \SI{7}{\percent} to
    spare at the tightest point.
  \item \textbf{Release.} On a result that guarantees its voltage's requirement
    (\cref{sec:pd-classes}) the MCU releases the display inhibit. On any other
    outcome it does not, and the board stays in limited operation for as long
    as that source is attached.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Why the display needs an inhibit at all.} The flyback's UVLO is a
voltage floor, not a contract check. It releases at \SI{4.4}{\volt} rising
(\cref{sec:hv-uvlo}), below every contract the window admits, so on any closed
contract it would start the display into whatever current allowance happens to
be in force, including the \SI{500}{\milli\ampere} the resistors ask at attach.
Nothing about the input voltage distinguishes a contract that carries the
display from one that does not, so the distinction has to be made by the only
element that knows the contract, and it must default to inhibited so that the
state before the MCU is running is the safe one.
\Cref{sec:hv-inhibit} derives it.

\paragraph{Step 4 is a current-only renegotiation.} With the controller
already holding the highest voltage in the window, step 4 is a current-only
renegotiation: the contract's voltage is unchanged, the pass switch stays
closed, and no capacitance is charged or discharged. A voltage step is the
hazardous operation. \Cref{sec:pd-fet} puts \SI{135}{\micro\farad} on the bus
rail, and during a negotiated voltage transition the pass switch is fully on and
cannot moderate the charging current the way it does at attach; at the source's
permitted transition slew that capacitance draws amperes. The design is built to
survive one such event, since the outcome is bounded: the source current-limits
or drops to its safe voltage, which returns to step~1 with the board in the
state it started in.

That distinction is what makes \cref{sec:pd-window}'s assumption recoverable. If
the controller does not in fact select the highest offered voltage, step 4
becomes a voltage step, the board takes one inrush event, and the MCU reaches
full operation anyway. The assumption's cost drops from a display that never
lights to a transient at start-up.

\paragraph{What step 4 writes.} A Request Data Object carries the selected
source capability and the operating current in one word, so revising the current
is an ordinary request. SINK\_RDO\_REQUEST takes
a 32-bit object and sends it to the source unexamined: ``Writing to this
register will result in Request of PD contract re-negotiation'' (HPI
specification, p.~24). The other route to the same contract, replacing the sink
capability list through SELECT\_SINK\_PDO, is rejected on the specification's own
warning that ``once the Sink PDOs are updated, BCR-PLUS will re-negotiate the PD
contract with the power adapter. This will cause momentary power loss to the
system'' (HPI specification, p.~22). Step 4 therefore writes an RDO and leaves
the capability list as the four resistors set it, which is also what keeps
\cref{sec:pd-window}'s window in force over a rejected request.

The register is gated on the sink PDO mask being non-zero, which it is at reset,
and its SUCCESS response means only that the request was queued. Whether the
contract closed is announced by the port's contract-negotiation-complete
response, whose payload carries the request the source accepted, and that is
what step 5 conditions on.

\subsection{Bus pass switch}\label{sec:pd-fet}

VBUS\_CTRL drives \des{Q}{BUS}'s gates through \des{R}{BUSG}, asserting only when
a contract inside the window closes.

\paragraph{The switch blocks in both directions, so it is two devices.} A single
P-channel MOSFET with its source on the receptacle's VBUS and its drain on
$V_{\mathrm{BUS}}$ blocks the forward path when it is off, but its body diode,
whose anode is the drain, conducts the reverse one. Two states make that
unacceptable. On detach the \SI{135}{\micro\farad} the bus rail carries, totalled
under \emph{Inrush} below, would drain onto the receptacle's exposed contacts
through that diode, against the requirement that VBUS fall to its safe level in a
bounded time. And an overvoltage on the port, the event for which the controller
opens the switch at all, would reach $V_{\mathrm{BUS}}$ regardless of the
switch's state, since the diode does not care that the channel is off.

\des{Q}{BUS} is therefore two P-channel devices with their sources joined and
their gates driven together. Each body diode has its anode on its own drain, so
the pair sits anode-to-anode at the shared source: current from either end
reaches that node through one diode and is blocked by the other. The common
source is also what lets one gate driver serve both, the gate-source voltage
being the same for each. The controller's own pin description assumes this
arrangement, directing DC\_OUT\_DIS to ``the output of the PFETs''
(\ds{infineon-cypd3176-24lqxq-datasheet-en.pdf}{9}).

\paragraph{Ratings.} Each device stands off the full contract voltage when the
pair is open, \SI{12.6}{\volt}, which with the \SI{20}{\percent} derating would
give $V_{DS} \geq \SI{16}{\volt}$. The binding figure is larger and comes from
elsewhere: \cref{sec:pd-esd} puts \SI{24}{\volt} on this node during an
electrostatic discharge, and raises the requirement to
$V_{DS} \geq \SI{30}{\volt}$. The gate-source junction is a separate question,
answered under \emph{Gate network} below, because the network holds it well
inside what the rail alone would impose. Unlike \cref{sec:3v3-diodeor}'s header
switch, this one faces no low-drive constraint: the gate has the whole bus rail
to work with, so threshold and on-resistance are not in tension here. Continuous
current is \cref{sec:pd-load}'s \SI{1.170}{\ampere}, giving $I_D \geq
\SI{1.5}{\ampere}$ derated.

\paragraph{On-resistance.} The drop across the switch comes out of the margin
between what the source presents and the \SI{4.75}{\volt} floor the downstream
derivations assume, and the two channels are in series so the budget is shared.
Specification: $R_{DS(\mathrm{on})} \leq \SI{25}{\milli\ohm}$ per device at the
gate drive the bus affords, \SI{50}{\milli\ohm} across the pair, which costs
\SI{59}{\milli\volt} at \SI{1.170}{\ampere} and \SI{68}{\milli\watt}.

\emph{Assumption:} the \SI{4.75}{\volt} floor is the voltage at
$V_{\mathrm{BUS}}$, and a \SI{5}{\volt} contract's $-5\,\%$ limit leaves no
budget above it at all. The switch's \SI{59}{\milli\volt} and the cable's
therefore come out of the floor itself: a compliant Type-C cable may present up
to \SI{250}{\milli\ohm} on the VBUS and ground conductors together, which at
\SI{1.170}{\ampere} is \SI{293}{\milli\volt}. A worst-case source, a worst-case
cable and a worst-case load together put $V_{\mathrm{BUS}}$ near
\SI{4.40}{\volt}, below the floor. The consequence is bounded: the flyback's
UVLO does not cross its falling threshold until \SI{4.29}{\volt}
(\cref{sec:hv-uvlo}), so the converter runs through the excursion, and a stack
that crosses the remaining \SI{110}{\milli\volt} returns the board to limited
operation through the UVLO rather than browning it out. Below the floor the
derivations stop being exact; the design degrades to a defined state rather
than an undefined one.

\paragraph{Inrush.} The bus rail's capacitance is the sum of the three
converters' input capacitance: \SI{100}{\micro\farad} polymer and
\SI{4.7}{\micro\farad} ceramic at the flyback (\cref{sec:hv-caps}),
\SI{10}{\micro\farad} at the 5~V converter (\cref{sec:5v-cin}), and
\SI{20}{\micro\farad} across the 3.3~V converter's two input capacitors
(\cref{sec:3v3-cin}), \SI{135}{\micro\farad} nominal. Charging it is a load the
source sees on top of the operating draw, and it happens at attach, under the
\SI{500}{\milli\ampere} contract the resistors request. Above
\cref{sec:pd-classes}'s \SI{0.404}{\ampere} that leaves \SI{96}{\milli\ampere}:
\begin{align*}
  t_{\mathrm{ramp}} &\geq \frac{C\,\Delta V}{I_{\mathrm{inrush}}}
    = \frac{\SI{135}{\micro\farad}\times\SI{12.6}{\volt}}{\SI{0.096}{\ampere}}
    = \SI{17.7}{\milli\second}.
\end{align*}
Specification: $t_{\mathrm{ramp}} \geq \SI{18}{\milli\second}$. The gate network
below is what sets it.

\paragraph{Gate network.} The gate driver by itself does none of three things
this switch requires: hold the pair off before the controller is powered, bound
the gate-source voltage, or set the output ramp. Five elements around the gate
node do, in the arrangement the manufacturer's application diagram carries
(\ds{infineon-cypd3176-24lqxq-datasheet-en.pdf}{12}).

\begin{description}
  \item[\des{R}{BUSOFF}, gate to common source] holds $V_{GS}$ at zero whenever
    nothing drives the gate: before the controller has powered up, through its
    reset, and on any fault that releases VBUS\_CTRL. The driver's own
    \SI{4}{\kilo\ohm} pull-up cannot serve here because it does not exist until
    the controller does, which is after the rail it gates. Specification:
    \SI{49.9}{\kilo\ohm}, large enough that the \SI{200}{\micro\ampere} it
    carries at full gate drive is a small part of what the driver must sink,
    small enough that gate leakage across it is negligible.
  \item[\des{R}{BUSBIAS}, gate to the receptacle's VBUS] lifts the off state
    above zero. With the pair open the common source rests
    one body-diode drop below the receptacle, so \des{R}{BUSBIAS} and
    \des{R}{BUSOFF} divide that drop onto the gate and leave $V_{GS}$ at about
    $+\SI{0.6}{\volt}$. It also gives the gate a defined path to the input rail
    during plug-in, before the source node has charged through the body diode at
    all. Specification: \SI{10}{\kilo\ohm}.
  \item[\des{D}{BUSG}, cathode on the source, anode on the gate] bounds
    $V_{GS}$. It is a protective element: with the driver's
    \SI{3}{\kilo\ohm} pull-down working against \des{R}{BUSBIAS} and
    \des{R}{BUSOFF}, the gate settles at
    \begin{align*}
      V_G &= V_S\,\frac{G_{\mathrm{BIAS}}+G_{\mathrm{OFF}}}
                       {G_{\mathrm{BIAS}}+G_{\mathrm{OFF}}+G_{\mathrm{PD}}}
        = 12.6 \times 0.324 = \SI{4.1}{\volt},
    \end{align*}
    that is $V_{GS} = -\SI{8.5}{\volt}$, short of the clamp. The clamp therefore
    does not conduct at this rail's voltages and exists for the excursions
    beyond them. Specification: \SI{10}{\volt} Zener, which caps the
    gate-source requirement on \des{Q}{BUS} at $|V_{GS}| \geq
    \SI{12.5}{\volt}$ with the \SI{20}{\percent} derating, where the bare rail
    would demand \SI{16}{\volt}.
  \item[\des{C}{BUSSLEW}, gate to $V_{\mathrm{BUS}}$] sets the output ramp. It
    is the Miller path: as the gate is drawn down the output rises against it, so
    $dV_{\mathrm{BUS}}/dt = I_{PD}/C$. Across the driver's current-mode range
    that is \SIrange{0.025}{0.58}{\volt\per\milli\second}, and at the strongest
    setting the \SI{12.6}{\volt} ramp takes \SI{21.7}{\milli\second} and draws
    $\SI{135}{\micro\farad}\times\SI{0.58}{\volt\per\milli\second} =
    \SI{78}{\milli\ampere}$ of inrush, inside the \SI{96}{\milli\ampere} budget
    above.
    Specification: \SI{1}{\micro\farad}.
  \item[\des{C}{BUSCM}, gate to the receptacle's VBUS] couples a fast transient
    on the input rail onto the gate and the source alike, so $V_{GS}$ holds
    through it and the switch cannot be driven by an edge on its own supply.
    Specification: \SI{1}{\micro\farad}.
\end{description}

\des{R}{BUSG} sits between VBUS\_CTRL and that node. It carries the whole of the
driver's pull-down and sets the current through \des{D}{BUSG} whenever the clamp
conducts. Specification: \des{R}{BUSG} $= \SI{1}{\kilo\ohm}$.

The drive-strength setting is therefore a design parameter. At the weakest of
the five, \SI{25}{\micro\ampere}, the same \SI{1}{\micro\farad} stretches the
ramp to \SI{500}{\milli\second}, which is long against the timing a source
expects a sink to settle in. It must be selected together with
\des{C}{BUSSLEW}.

\subsection{Bus-rail discharge}\label{sec:pd-bleed}

\Cref{sec:3v3-diodeor} derives a bound on the impedance at $V_{\mathrm{BUS}}$,
$R_{\mathrm{BUSBLEED}} \leq \SI{287}{\kilo\ohm}$ against \des{Q}{3V3BUS}'s
off-state drain leakage, and leaves open whether the front end satisfies it as a
by-product of its own detach behavior. It does not.

The controller carries an integrated VBUS discharge FET, described as
``used to discharge VBUS to meet the USB-PD specification timing on a detach
condition'' (\ds{infineon-cypd3176-24lqxq-datasheet-en.pdf}{5}). That is an
event-driven function with a timing requirement behind it, and the datasheet
specifies no static impedance at either VBUS\_IN\_DIS or DC\_OUT\_DIS. The
requirement \cref{sec:3v3-diodeor} raises is a continuous one: while the board
runs from the header branch, \des{Q}{3V3BUS}'s
drain leakage flows into a bus rail that no source is holding, and the rail
must rest near ground for as long as that lasts. A discharge that fires once on
detach does not hold it there.

\des{R}{BUSBLEED} is therefore populated. Its value
follows from \des{Q}{3V3BUS}'s off-state drain leakage at the maximum ambient,
which \cref{sec:3v3-diodeor} puts at \SI{2.3}{\micro\ampere}. Its cost while a
bus supply is attached is $V_{\mathrm{BUS}}^2/R$, which at \SI{13.0}{\volt} and
the specified \SI{100}{\kilo\ohm} is \SI{1.69}{\milli\watt} and
\SI{130}{\micro\ampere} against \cref{sec:pd-load}'s budget.

\subsection{Data path}\label{sec:pd-data}

D+ and D$-$ reach three places: the controller's own D+ and D$-$ pins, which
carry BC1.2, AFC, QC~2.0 and Apple negotiation for sources that do not implement
USB-PD; the ESD array; and the MCU's native USB (\cref{sec:esp32-usb}). USB~2.0
admits one host and one device on a segment, so the third connection is
switched.

\paragraph{Legacy negotiation reaches the contracts the display needs.} QC~2.0 signals its
\SI{9}{\volt} and \SI{12}{\volt} steps on D+ and D$-$, which are exactly the
contracts \cref{sec:pd-window} accepts. A source implementing QC but not PD can
therefore run the display, and the negotiation sequence reaches it before
falling through to Type-C-only. This is why CHARGING\_MODE selects the full set
of legacy protocols (\cref{sec:pd-pins}), and why the controller's D+/D$-$
connection is required.

\paragraph{The two claims on the pair are separable in time.}
QC~2.0 and AFC do not negotiate and release. They express the contract as a
state the sink holds on D+ and D$-$ for as long as it wants the voltage, so a
sink that surrenders the pair surrenders the contract with it. The controller
and the MCU therefore cannot both have the pair while such a contract is in
force, and no arrangement of switches changes that; the conflict is over who
holds the signal.

What makes the design work is that the conflict never arises in a case the board
meets. The MCU wants the pair to enumerate against a host, and a host port
offers PD or Type-C-only, never QC or AFC. A source that offers QC or AFC is a
charger, and a charger is not a host. The two demands are disjoint over the
population of sources, and the front end only has to know which one it is
looking at.

CHARGING\_MODE\_STATUS answers that question, reporting no legacy mode, BC1.2,
QC~2.0, AFC or Apple as a single byte (HPI specification, p.~18). The MCU reads
it before closing the switch and closes only on the first two: no legacy mode
means the contract came from PD or from Type-C-only and nothing is holding the
pair, and a BC1.2 result means a charging port whose D+ and D$-$ are shorted
together at the source, which cannot be a host in any case and cannot be
disturbed by a device that fails to enumerate against it. On QC~2.0, AFC or
Apple the switch stays open, and the board is a clock without a console.

This condition lives in firmware. It is derived here because it is what permits
the controller and the MCU to share one pair at all, and because it is the
reason \cref{sec:pd-pins} can leave CHARGING\_MODE floating without
qualification.

\paragraph{The switch goes in the MCU's branch alone.} The controller needs the
pair at attach, running from the receptacle's VBUS through its own regulator,
which is before a contract closes, before \des{Q}{BUS} turns on, and therefore
before the 3.3~V rail exists. Any switch powered from that rail is unpowered at
that moment. A single-throw switch in the MCU's branch makes the unpowered state
the correct one: open, with the controller holding the pair. The MCU closes it
once it is running and wants to enumerate. A two-way multiplexer at the junction
would need a supply that exists before the contract does and a resistor-defined
default pointing at the controller, to reach a state the series switch gets for
nothing.

The MCU's own pins argue the same way from the other end. Its USB pins are
general-purpose I/O with clamps to a rail that does not exist during
negotiation, so an unswitched MCU branch would load D+ and D$-$ exactly while
the controller is driving them to \SI{0.6}{\volt} or \SI{3.3}{\volt} against a
charger. And both pins glitch high twice at power-up, for about
\SI{60}{\micro\second} each within windows of \SI{3.2}{\milli\second} and
\SI{2}{\milli\second} (\cref{sec:esp32-usb}), which the switch confines to the
MCU side of itself.

Specification: the enable net is held in the disabled state by \des{R}{USBSW}
against a high-impedance MCU pin, so the switch is open through reset and
opens again if the MCU stops driving it; the MCU pin carrying it must be free of
strapping duty (\cref{sec:esp32-usb}). The switch's select input, where
the part has one, is tied to the level that reaches the populated branch.

\paragraph{Orientation.} A USB~2.0-only receptacle ties A6/A7 to B6/B7, so the
pair is present in both cable orientations and no orientation switching is
required. The FLIP output the controller offers on DATAMODE/FLIP is therefore
unused; \cref{sec:pd-pins} keeps that pin in its data-capable configuration for
the other half of its function.

\paragraph{Capacitance on the shared node.} The pair carries the receptacle, the
ESD array, the controller's two pins, the switch's common side and, behind the
switch, the MCU. The USB~2.0 specification bounds the total at
\SI{100}{\pico\farad} on the upstream port of a device with a detachable cable,
against \SI{150}{\pico\farad} on a downstream-facing one. The contributors, each
at its worst case:

\paragraph{The controller's branch.} \des{R}{PDDP} and \des{R}{PDDM} join the
controller's D+ and D$-$ to the pair. At \SI{0}{\ohm} they are the connection
\cref{sec:pd-esd} protects and this subsection's argument assumes.

\emph{Assumption:} the controller leaves D+ and D$-$ high-impedance when no
legacy protocol is active. The pins are ordinary GPIO, numbered among the port
pins and specified in the GPIO table with the GPIO pull resistors
(\ds{infineon-cypd3176-24lqxq-datasheet-en.pdf}{19}), and the datasheet
contemplates a data application on this receptacle, but it nowhere states the
idle behavior. If the assumption fails the controller holds the pair
permanently, no firmware condition recovers it, and the recourse is to depopulate
\des{R}{PDDP} and \des{R}{PDDM}: the controller then loses the legacy protocols
it cannot run without the lines, the data path runs from receptacle to switch
alone, and every source that offers PD or Type-C-only, which is every source the
board is programmed from, behaves as derived. The assumption's cost is the
high-voltage legacy protocols, which is why it is worth taking.

\subsection{ESD protection}\label{sec:pd-esd}

The receptacle's contacts are exposed metal and require IEC 61000-4-2
protection independent of what the silicon behind them carries. The five lines
do not take the same treatment, and the reason is that their standoff
requirements differ by more than a factor of four: D+ and D$-$ never leave the
MCU's logic rail, CC rests near ground, and VBUS carries the negotiated
contract. No single device spans that range, so the protection divides three
ways.

The controller's own ESD table makes the first cut
(\ds{infineon-cypd3176-24lqxq-datasheet-en.pdf}{16}). Contact discharge at
\SI{8}{\kilo\volt} is specified on CC1, CC2 and VBUS\_IN\_DIS; D+ and D$-$
appear only under air discharge.

\paragraph{CC1 and CC2 take nothing.} The controller specifies both against the
harder of the two tests, and nothing else on the board connects to them. An
external clamp would add capacitance to the lines carrying the power-delivery
physical layer for no protection the controller does not already provide.

\paragraph{D+ and D$-$ take a matched array.} These are the lines the
controller does \emph{not} specify for contact discharge, and they run on to the
analog switch of \cref{sec:pd-data} and the MCU of \cref{sec:esp32-usb}, neither
of which carries an IEC rating of any kind. Requirement: IEC 61000-4-2 level 4,
no more than \SI{4}{\pico\farad} per line, which is the share of
\cref{tab:pd-datacap}'s budget an array can be held to without difficulty, and
line-to-line capacitance matched so the pair stays balanced. The last of those
is what a discrete device per line cannot deliver; matching requires a single
die.

\des{D}{USBESD}, the USBLC6-2 (\texttt{USBLC6-2.pdf}, STMicroelectronics),
satisfies all three: \SI{3.5}{\pico\farad} maximum line capacitance,
\SI{0.015}{\pico\farad} matching between the two I/O lines,
\SI{150}{\nano\ampere} maximum leakage, and \SI{8}{\kilo\volt} contact with
\SI{15}{\kilo\volt} air discharge, in a SOT-666 or SOT23-6L body.

Its rail pin does not connect to the receptacle's VBUS. That pin is the anode
rail for the steering diodes with a suppressor behind it, specified at
$V_{RM} = \SI{5.25}{\volt}$ and a breakdown between it and ground of
\SI{6}{\volt} minimum (\ds{USBLC6-2.pdf}{2}); the part is a USB~2.0 device built
for a \SI{5}{\volt} bus, and a \SI{9}{\volt} or \SI{12}{\volt} contract would
hold that junction past breakdown continuously. Specification: the rail pin ties
to VDDD, which is inside the standoff at \SI{3.3}{\volt} and, unlike any
converter output, is alive as soon as the receptacle has voltage on it.

\paragraph{VBUS takes its own clamp.} The controller's VBUS\_IN\_DIS pin is
rated for contact discharge, but it is not the only thing on that node:
\des{Q}{BUS} sits directly across from the receptacle and carries no IEC rating,
and \des{C}{PDIN} and the gate network hang off the same rail. Requirement:
a suppressor with a standoff above the \SI{12.6}{\volt} maximum contract,
IEC 61000-4-2 level 4, and a clamping voltage low enough that what it lets
through is survivable by everything else on the node.

It must be unidirectional. This is a ground-referenced DC rail that never goes
negative, and VBUS\_IN\_DIS has an absolute minimum of $-\SI{0.3}{\volt}$. A
unidirectional device clamps a negative discharge at one forward drop; a
bidirectional one avalanches in both directions and would not clamp until its
full breakdown voltage below ground.

\des{D}{BUSTVS}, the SD15Q (\texttt{SD15Q.pdf}, Diodes Incorporated), meets it:
\SI{15}{\volt} standoff against \SI{12.6}{\volt} of operating voltage,
\SI{16}{\volt} minimum breakdown, \SI{1}{\micro\ampere} maximum leakage at the
full standoff, and $\pm\SI{30}{\kilo\volt}$ contact and air discharge to
IEC 61000-4-2, in SOD-323.

Its clamping behavior has to be read from the right test. The tabulated figures,
\SI{21}{\volt} at \SI{1}{\ampere} rising to \SI{27}{\volt} at
\SI{12}{\ampere}, are 8/20~\si{\micro\second} surge measurements whose apparent
dynamic resistance includes self-heating across a \SI{20}{\micro\second} pulse.
An electrostatic discharge is three orders of magnitude shorter and the die does
not heat, so the governing figure is the transmission-line-pulse curve at
\SI{100}{\nano\second} (\ds{SD15Q.pdf}{3}): it turns on near
\SI{19.5}{\volt} and reaches \SI{24}{\volt} at \SI{30}{\ampere}, the first-peak
current an \SI{8}{\kilo\volt} contact discharge produces. Roughly
\SI{0.15}{\ohm} of dynamic resistance, against \SI{0.55}{\ohm} inferred from the
surge table.

\paragraph{The clamp voltage is a requirement on everything else on the node.}
\SI{24}{\volt} is what the rest of the rail must survive during an event. The
\SI{20}{\percent} derating this document applies elsewhere does not apply here:
a clamp is a transient, and the requirement is only that an absolute maximum not
be exceeded. Three consequences follow.
\begin{description}
  \item[\des{Q}{BUS}] needs $V_{DS} \geq \SI{24}{\volt}$, which the
    \SI{16}{\volt} of \cref{sec:pd-fet} does not cover. Specification:
    \SI{30}{\volt}, the next standard grade. On-resistance at that grade
    remains inside the \SI{25}{\milli\ohm} the same subsection
    requires, so nothing else moves.
  \item[\des{C}{PDIN}] sees the clamp across it. A \SI{25}{\volt} part is
    under-rated for a \SI{24}{\volt} transient. Specification:
    \SI{50}{\volt}, available in 0603 at no change in body size.
  \item[VBUS\_IN\_DIS] has a \SI{30}{\volt} absolute maximum, used to
    \SI{80}{\percent} at the clamp. That is the thinnest margin the clamp
    leaves, and it is on the one pin that carries its own
    \SI{8}{\kilo\volt} rating and therefore does not depend on this device.
\end{description}

\subsection{Configuration and housekeeping pins}\label{sec:pd-pins}

\paragraph{CHARGING\_MODE.} Left floating, which selects all legacy charging
protocols; a \SI{5}{\kilo\ohm} pull-up to VDDD would select BC1.2 only.
\Cref{sec:pd-data} gives the reason: QC~2.0 reaches the \SI{9}{\volt} and
\SI{12}{\volt} contracts this design wants, and BC1.2 alone reaches only
\SI{5}{\volt}. No component.

The pin is a strap. It is sampled once, at power-up, to fix
which protocols the controller will attempt
(\ds{infineon-cy4534-bcr-plus-usermanual-en.pdf}{14}), so the choice made here
is the choice for the whole attachment. Its run-time counterpart is
CHARGING\_\allowbreak MODE\_\allowbreak DISABLE, which withdraws legacy charging
altogether (HPI specification, p.~30). Nothing in this design writes it: the only moment it
would help is one in which CHARGING\_MODE\_STATUS has already reported that no
legacy protocol is active, and there is then nothing to withdraw.

\paragraph{DATAMODE/FLIP.} Left unconnected, which is the configuration for
``applications that need to sink power as well as support USB communication''
(\ds{infineon-cypd3176-24lqxq-datasheet-en.pdf}{9}). The alternative, a
\SI{1}{\kilo\ohm} pull-up, declares the application to be power-only and is
incompatible with \cref{sec:pd-data}. No component.

The datasheet's own data-capable application diagram pairs this setting with a
controller whose D+ and D$-$ are unconnected and with the BC1.2-only strap on
CHARGING\_MODE, which is the arrangement that results from declining to arbitrate
the pair at all. \Cref{sec:pd-data} arbitrates it instead, in firmware, and
keeps the protocols that arrangement gives up. The pin's own description
attaches no condition to the data setting beyond the one this design meets.

\paragraph{SAFE\_PWR\_EN.} Unused and unconnected, the window of
\cref{sec:pd-window} having made it inoperative.

\paragraph{FAULT.} Driven to the MCU. A weak pull-down to ground puts the pin in
strong-drive mode at \SI{3.3}{\volt}; a \SI{1.8}{\volt} pull-up would instead
select open-drain mode
(\ds{infineon-cypd3176-24lqxq-datasheet-en.pdf}{8}), which matches the MCU's
input levels without translation. Specification: \des{R}{PDFLT} $=
\SI{100}{\kilo\ohm}$ to ground. The signal reports the controller's fault
detections, over-voltage, over-current and over-temperature among them, and it
is the one piece of front-end state that reaches the MCU without a bus
transaction: the contract questions of \cref{sec:pd-classes} are answered over
HPI, but a fault can invalidate what HPI reports, and the pin is the path that
does not depend on it.

\paragraph{The same pin carries an indicator.} Strong-drive mode makes FAULT a
push-pull output, so an indicator and the MCU input can share it, and the
indicator branch does not disturb \des{R}{PDFLT}: below the diode's forward
voltage that branch is an open, which is where the mode select reads. What
bounds the arrangement is the pin's drive. It guarantees
$V_{OH} \geq V_{DDD} - \SI{0.6}{\volt}$ at \SI{4}{\milli\ampere}
(\ds{infineon-cypd3176-24lqxq-datasheet-en.pdf}{19}), an output resistance of
\SI{150}{\ohm}, and the MCU reads a high above
$0.75\,V_{DD} = \SI{2.475}{\volt}$ (\cref{sec:esp32-mcu}). Loading the pin to
its full rating leaves \SI{2.7}{\volt} and only \SI{225}{\milli\volt} of margin,
so the indicator takes a fraction of it:
\begin{align*}
  I &= \frac{V_{DDD} - V_F}{R + \SI{150}{\ohm}}, &
  V_{OH} &= V_{DDD} - \SI{150}{\ohm}\cdot I,
\end{align*}
which at $R = \SI{1}{\kilo\ohm}$ and a red diode's \SI{1.9}{\volt} gives
\SI{1.22}{\milli\ampere} and \SI{3.12}{\volt}, \SI{0.65}{\volt} of margin at
under a third of the rating. Specification: \des{R}{PDIND} $\geq
\SI{1}{\kilo\ohm}$, and the indicator is a red or amber diode. A blue or white
one forward-drops close to the pin's own high level and would light weakly or
not at all.

\paragraph{HPI.} HPI\_SDA, HPI\_SCL and HPI\_INT go to the MCU as an I\textsuperscript{2}C
slave at address 0x08, all three open-drain and pulled up to the 3.3~V rail:
\des{R}{PDSDA}, \des{R}{PDSCL}, \des{R}{PDINT} $= \SI{10}{\kilo\ohm}$, the value
the datasheet recommends for HPI\_INT. The interface is required: it is the
route by which \cref{sec:pd-sequence} raises the contract from what the
resistors ask to what full operation needs, and the resistors cannot be sized to
do both.

\subparagraph{The pull-ups belong on the MCU's rail.} The
two supplies do not come up together. VDDD is alive from attach, running off the
receptacle's VBUS through the controller's own regulator, while the 3.3~V rail
waits for a contract and for \des{Q}{BUS}. Pull-ups referred to VDDD would
therefore hold three lines at \SI{3.3}{\volt} against an MCU whose rail is at
zero, and its pin clamps would conduct into that dead rail. Referred to the
3.3~V rail, the bus rests at ground through the same interval and no pin is
driven against an unpowered die. Nothing is lost: every use of the interface
falls after negotiation, when both supplies are up. The controller's side needs
no pull-up of its own, its pins being open-drain and idle.

\subparagraph{The value.} A pull-up sets the rise time against the bus
capacitance, $t_r = 0.847RC$ between the \SI{30}{\percent} and
\SI{70}{\percent} thresholds, and fast mode allows \SI{300}{\nano\second}. The
bus is two devices and a short trace: \SI{7}{\pico\farad} at the controller's
pins (\cref{tab:cypd3176}), a comparable figure at the MCU's, and the trace,
call it \SI{27}{\pico\farad} in total. \SI{10}{\kilo\ohm} then gives
\SI{229}{\nano\second}, inside the limit, at the controller's maximum
\SI{400}{\kilo\bit\per\second}. The \SI{2.2}{\kilo\ohm} of the reference
application is what a longer bus or a third device would need, and is not
required here.

\paragraph{Supply and decoupling.} VBUS\_IN\_DIS connects to the receptacle's
VBUS with \des{C}{PDIN} $= \SI{100}{\nano\farad}$, meeting the
\SI{0.1}{\micro\farad} X5R minimum. VDDD is the internal \SI{3.3}{\volt}
regulator's output and takes \des{C}{PDD1} $= \SI{1}{\micro\farad}$ against the
\SIrange{0.8}{1}{\micro\farad} requirement plus \des{C}{PDD2} and \des{C}{PDD3}
$= \SI{100}{\nano\farad}$. VCCD is the internal \SI{1.8}{\volt} core regulator's
output and takes \des{C}{PDC} $= \SI{1}{\micro\farad}$. VDDD supplies nothing
outside the controller other than the two divider pull-ups, whose combined draw
is \SI{0.98}{\milli\ampere} at \SI{3.3}{\volt}.

\paragraph{CC1, CC2.} Direct to the receptacle. The sink terminations are
internal at \SIrange{4.59}{5.61}{\kilo\ohm}, so no external Rd is required, and
the \SI{22}{\volt} absolute maximum covers any compliant source.

\paragraph{DC\_OUT\_DIS.} Connects to \des{Q}{BUS}'s output drain, that is, to
$V_{\mathrm{BUS}}$, which is what the pin monitors. Its \SI{30}{\volt} absolute
maximum is used to \SI{42}{\percent} at \SI{12.6}{\volt}.

\paragraph{DNU1, DNU2.} Left unconnected as the datasheet directs.

\paragraph{CSP.} Tied to VSS, forgoing the controller's sink over-current
protection. The function does real work: an over-current
trip turns the external pass FET off within \SI{50}{\micro\second}
(\ds{infineon-cypd3176-24lqxq-datasheet-en.pdf}{23}), with no host involvement,
and it also asserts FAULT. What it costs is the network it needs. The pin's
absolute maximum is VDDD~$+\SI{0.5}{\volt}$, so it cannot sense on the bus rail,
and the reference application senses in the return instead: a
\SI{5}{\milli\ohm} shunt separates the receptacle's ground from the board's,
CSP on the receptacle side and VSS on the board side
(\ds{infineon-cypd3176-24lqxq-datasheet-en.pdf}{11}). Every return current on
the board then crosses that element, including the discharge \cref{sec:pd-esd}
routes into the receptacle's ground, which has to stay on the receptacle side of
the shunt to keep an electrostatic event out of the board's reference. A split
ground plane is a large price for a protection the design does not need: the
negotiated contract bounds the draw at the source, each converter current-limits
at its own input, and a downstream short is answered by the source's own
protection at the cost of a renegotiation. The trip threshold is also a
configuration-table parameter, not pin-programmed, so the shunt does nothing
until the part is reprogrammed.

The front end's substantive result is that one setting, a \SI{5}{\volt} floor
on the accepted window, converts a board that is dead on any ordinary host port
into one that comes up on every source it can be plugged into, and does it with
no components at all: the setting is a connection to ground. What it costs is
that $V_{\mathrm{BUS}}$ is no longer guaranteed to carry the current the
display draws, and the design absorbs that without a new mechanism because the
converters are derived across the whole window and the one element that knows
the contract, the inhibit of \cref{sec:hv-inhibit}, exists precisely to gate
the display on current rather than voltage.
